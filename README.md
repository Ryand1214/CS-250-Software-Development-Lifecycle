CS 250 Software Development Lifecycle

This repository holds my Sprint Review and Retrospective from CS 250 at Southern New Hampshire University. The document looks back on the SNHU Travel project, a Scrum pilot at ChadaTech where our team built a travel application that helps users find vacation destinations based on their interests. I rotated through the Product Owner, Scrum Master, developer, and tester roles during the project, and in the retrospective I reflect on how each role contributed, how user stories guided our work, how the team adjusted when requirements changed mid sprint, and how the Agile approach compared to a more traditional waterfall model.

Reflection
How do I interpret user needs and implement them into a program? How does creating user stories help with this?

I start by listening to what the user is actually trying to accomplish instead of jumping straight to features. A client might ask for a specific screen or button, but what they really care about is the outcome, like a customer being able to find a wellness or detox vacation without digging through dozens of listings. Once I understand that goal, I can work backward to the functionality that supports it.

User stories make this much easier because they force the requirement into the user's point of view. Writing something in the format of "As a traveler, I want to filter destinations by activity so that I can find trips that match what I enjoy" keeps the focus on who the feature is for and why it matters. Our backlog had seven stories, and pairing each one with measurable acceptance criteria gave our team a shared definition of done, so the developers and testers knew exactly what success looked like before anyone wrote code. User stories also break large requests into small pieces that fit inside a sprint, which made it possible to estimate the work and deliver value to the client in steady increments.

How do I approach developing programs? What Agile processes do I hope to incorporate into my future development work?

I try to approach development in small, testable steps rather than trying to build everything at once. I would rather get a working piece in front of someone early, get feedback, and adjust than spend weeks building something that misses the mark. This course showed me why that mindset matters. When the SNHU Travel client shifted direction partway through the project, the team was able to reprioritize the backlog and keep moving because we were working in short sprints instead of following a fixed plan from start to finish.

Going forward I want to bring several Agile practices into my own work. Daily standups are something I already value in my role at New York Life, because a quick check on progress and blockers keeps small problems from turning into big ones. I also want to keep using sprint planning with a prioritized backlog, writing tests alongside the code so quality is built in rather than added at the end (testing during the sprint is how we caught a broken image caused by a filename case mismatch before it reached the client), and holding retrospectives so the team regularly looks at what is working and what needs to change. The retrospective in particular stuck with me, since it turns improvement into a habit instead of something that only happens after a project goes wrong.

What does it mean to be a good team member in software development?

To me, being a good team member means being reliable, communicating openly, and caring about the team's goal more than my own individual piece. In a Scrum team the Product Owner, Scrum Master, developers, and testers all depend on each other, so if I am stuck on something I need to say so early instead of quietly falling behind. It also means being honest in reviews and retrospectives, giving feedback respectfully, and being willing to hear feedback about my own work without getting defensive.

A good team member is also flexible. Requirements change, priorities shift, and sometimes the most helpful thing I can do is pick up a task outside my usual role, like helping test a feature or clarifying a user story with the Product Owner. Agile works because the whole team shares responsibility for delivering something the client actually needs, and I want to be the kind of teammate who makes that easier for everyone around me.
